Cost, aid & value

What does university cost — and what can it return?

University is a major investment. This page puts the two sides of that decision together: what you may spend, what can reduce the cost, and the kinds of employment, earnings and career outcomes a degree can help make possible. No magic payback number. No pretending the cost is trivial. Just a clearer picture of the investment.

A degree is more than first salary.

The value can include access to occupations, advancement, professional pathways, networks, work experience, adaptability and further study.
Longer horizon

One year's cost versus decades of working life

Long-term evidence matters because many degrees do not reveal their full economic value in the first job after graduation.

20-year earnings premium

Statistics Canada longitudinal research found a large earnings premium associated with bachelor's completion versus high school over a 20-year period. The study also cautions that results reflect a specific cohort and should not be read as a guaranteed causal return.

Career resilience

The same longitudinal work found bachelor's graduates had more years with employer-sponsored pension coverage and fewer layoffs than high-school graduates in the cohort studied.

Five- and ten-year outcomes

Statistics Canada now publishes median employment-income data by field of study at five and ten years after graduation, creating a strong basis for field-specific ROI views in a production version.

Ways to improve the equation

Reduce the cost. Strengthen the return.

Scholarships & bursaries

Lower the upfront investment and make the financing plan more manageable.

Co-op & paid experience

Earn while studying, build experience earlier and strengthen the transition into employment.

Career-building choices

Research, internships, leadership, networks and complementary skills can materially shape what happens after graduation.
